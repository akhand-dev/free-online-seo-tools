The Statistics and Probabilities Behind Coin Flipping

A coin flip (also known as a coin toss) is a classic binary probability game where a flat object is spun in the air, landing on one of two possible outcomes: Heads or Tails. Coin tosses are commonly used to resolve disputes, start athletic games, or demonstrate statistical models.

The Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

In probability theory, flipping a balanced coin represents a trial with a strictly equal probability of 50% (0.5) for each outcome. If you flip a coin 10 times, you might get 7 Heads and 3 Tails (a 70% Heads draw rate). However, the **Law of Large Numbers** states that as the number of trials increases, the actual observed percentage converges closer to the expected theoretical probability of 50%. By using our Batch Toss Simulator to run 1,000 coin flips instantly, you can watch this mathematical principle in action.

Theoretical vs. Empirical Probability

Empirical probability refers to the actual ratio of outcomes observed during a physical experiment, whereas theoretical probability is calculated purely mathematically. Our simulator bridges these concepts, allowing students and developers to compare experimental runs against theoretical expectations instantly.
